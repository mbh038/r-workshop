Errors

Type One and Type Two

Type I error (false positive)


This occurs when a researcher claims to detect an effect or relationship that does not actually exist.

Misidentifying Pollution Impacts


A study claims that a new coastal construction project (e.g., an offshore wind farm) significantly reduces local fish diversity.

  • Type I Error:
    • The observed decline might actually be due to natural fluctuations (e.g., seasonal migration or temperature changes), but the study attributes it to the construction.
  • Cause:
    • Insufficient baseline data or short-term monitoring that doesn’t account for natural variability.

Species Detection Errors:


A study uses camera traps to assess whether a critically endangered leopard subspecies is extinct.

  • Type 1 Error:
    • Researchers conclude the subspecies still exists—when it is actually gone
  • Cause:
    • Poor camera placement or a fleeting misidentification (e.g., a similar-looking common leopard)
  • Consequence: Conservation efforts (funding, patrols) might be directed to a “ghost” population instead of other urgent needs.

False Detection of Species Invasions


Researchers report a “new” invasive crab species on an artificial reef.

  • Type I Error:
    • The crab might be a rare native species misidentified due to limited taxonomic expertise or atypical habitat use (e.g., displaced by storms).
  • Cause:
    • Sampling bias (e.g., only surveying the reef edge) or lack of genetic confirmation.

Habitat Impact Studies:


A team studies logging impacts on bird diversity, finds no birds and concludes that logging does have an impact on diversity

  • Type I Error:
    • This was just a fluke in sampling timing.
  • Cause:
    • The researchers sampled a patch that birds had temporariliy vacated due to a storm.

Overestimating Climate Change Effects


A study concludes that coral bleaching in a specific reef is caused by rising sea temperatures.

  • Type I Error:
    • The bleaching might actually stem from a local stressor (e.g., sediment runoff or disease), but the broader climate narrative dominates interpretation.
  • Cause:
    • Confounding variables not measured or controlled.

Faulty Conservation Priorities


A paper claims a marine protected area (MPA) is failing because fish sizes decreased.

  • Type I Error:
    • The decline could reflect normal population cycling or sampling artifacts (e.g., larger fish avoiding divers), not MPA ineffectiveness.
  • Cause:
    • Small sample sizes or non-random sampling (e.g., only surveying easily accessible sites).

Misleading Behavioral Studies


A study suggests that underwater noise from ships alters dolphin communication.

  • Type I Error:
    • The dolphins’ behavior might vary naturally (e.g., due to prey availability), but noise is blamed without proper controls.
  • Cause:
    • Lack of pre-noise baseline data or pseudoreplication (repeating observations on the same group).

How to Minimize Type I Errors:

  • Increase sample size/replication to capture natural variability.

  • Use controls (e.g., comparing impacted sites to unaffected “control” sites).

  • Long-term monitoring to distinguish trends from noise.

  • Statistical rigour: Adjust significance thresholds (e.g., Bonferroni correction) for multiple comparisons.

Type II error (false negative)


This occurs when a researcher fails to detect an effect or relationship that actually exists.

Missed Species Detection

A camera trap survey concludes that a rare carnivore is absent from a forest

  • Type II Error:
    • In reality, the species is present.
  • Cause:
    • The carnivore went undetected due to poor placement of cameras or insufficient survey duration.

Underestimating Population Decline


A bird population monitoring study finds no significant decline in numbers, leading to the conclusion that the population is stable.

  • Type II Error:
    • In reality, the population is in decline.
  • Cause:
    • The survey was conducted outside the breeding season when the birds were less visible, masking the real decline.

Ineffective Conservation Action


A study evaluates the impact of a marine protected area (MPA) on fish populations and finds no increase in fish abundance.

  • Type II Error:
    • In fact, there is a real decline in fish numbers.
  • Cause:
    • The study period was too short to detect long-term effects, leading to the false conclusion that MPAs are ineffective.

How to Minimize Type II Errors:

  • Failure to account for confounding variables to capture natural variability.

  • Low statistical power (This is often due to sample sizes being too samll, but could also arise from high variability of the target population.
